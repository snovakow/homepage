VisibleHuman

The Visible Human Project's male and female bodies, and its additional head, in 3D in the browser. Turn them, cut them open along any of the three anatomical planes, and see them as Gaussian splats, an X-ray, traced surfaces or meshes.

The Bodies

The U.S. National Library of Medicine's Visible Human Project photographed two bodies in cross-sections, from the top of the head to the soles of the feet: the male's a millimetre apart, and the female's a third of a millimetre. Nothing was sliced, strictly: each body was frozen in a block of gelatin and ground away from the top down, and the newly bared face of the block was photographed after every pass. A head, frozen on its own in ice, was photographed in cross-sections too, nearly seven to the millimetre. Each was scanned by CT and MRI before it was sectioned.

Stacked back up, the photographs make a volume in true colour, and the CT scans give every part of it a density.

The male was a man of 39, the female a woman of 59, and the head came from a man of 72.

From Photographs to Volume

The NLM's photographs come to 5.7 GB for the male and over 16 GB for the female, far too much for a page to load, and its server doesn't let other sites read them. So a Node script reads them once and writes a compact volume file for each body: about 5.7 MB for the male, 4.6 MB for the female and 10.8 MB for the head, at the levels the page opens on.

  • It tells the body apart from the blue gel it was set in, and from the frame, scale card and labels around it, then averages what's left into blocks, each keeping the colour of the tissue in it.
  • It registers each body's CT onto the photographs by fitting the CT's bone onto the pale bone in them, so that the blocks take their density from the CT, or from their colour where the scan doesn't reach.
  • The head, set in ice rather than gel, has a rule of its own for telling tissue from ice. Its CT is fitted with a single turn and shift, and its MRI onto the CT by mutual information.
  • Finer levels, 2 mm for the bodies and 1 mm for the head, are built when the site is deployed, and can be chosen in the app.

Four Ways to Draw It

  • Splats draw each block of the body as a soft blob of its colour, up to about 1.6 million of them, laid over one another in order, added together as a glow, or weighted with no order at all.
  • Volume follows a ray from the eye through each pixel and samples the body along it: laying the tissue's colours over one another, showing how much of an X-ray the body would stop, or picking out the densest tissue along each ray, which shows the skeleton. Its density comes from the CT, from the colour, or for the head from its MRI.
  • Raytrace follows the same ray to the skin, the bone or the head's brain, and lights the surface it finds there, with layers of see-through skin and cast shadows.
  • Surface builds the skin and the bone as meshes in a background thread, coloured from the photographs, with a see-through skin, order-independent transparency, and caps that show the section on each cut.

In every style, axial, coronal and sagittal cuts open the body to show its inside.

Built With

TypeScript, React, three.js and React Three Fiber, built with Vite, on WebGPU with a WebGL 2 fallback. The splats use three.js's built-in Gaussian splats, and the other styles are written in its node materials. Node.js scripts fetch the NLM's images and build each body's volume from them, reading the photographs with sharp and the head's scans with dicom-parser.
